## 3. Policy Options

### Option 1: AI Skills and Transition Accounts
A national AI Skills and Transition Account would provide targeted training support to workers in occupations experiencing significant AI-related task change. Eligible workers could receive a government-funded training credit that could be used for accredited short courses, micro credentials or vocational education focused on AI literacy, digital skills and complementary human capabilities such as communication, judgement and problem-solving.

The policy would primarily target workers in occupations identified by DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia as having relatively high exposure to AI-driven task transformation. Support could also be prioritised toward workers with limited access to employer-funded training, including lower-income workers, casual employees and workers in regions with fewer alternative employment opportunities.

The economic mechanism is to reduce the private cost of retraining and improve labour mobility. By helping workers develop skills that complement rather than compete directly with AI, the policy could reduce adjustment costs while allowing firms to continue adopting productivity-enhancing technologies.

For policy comparison purposes, an initial pilot could be modelled at approximately $250–400 million over three years. This is an illustrative analytical assumption rather than an existing government costing and would need to be refined using expected participant numbers, training costs and administrative expenses.

### Option 2: Employer Co-Investment for AI Adoption and Worker Training
This option would provide co-funding to employers that adopt AI while also investing in workforce training and job redesign. Eligible firms could receive grants or tax-based support for approved AI implementation costs, but access would be conditional on providing employees with relevant training, consultation and redeployment opportunities.

The policy would primarily target small and medium-sized enterprises, which may face greater financial and capability constraints when adopting AI than large firms. Requiring worker training as a condition of support would help ensure that public funding does not simply subsidise technology purchases, but also strengthens the skills needed to use AI productively.

The economic mechanism is to address possible underinvestment in both technology adoption and worker training. Firms may be reluctant to invest in employee skills if workers can later move to other employers, while smaller businesses may also face upfront costs and uncertainty when introducing new technologies. Co-investment can reduce these barriers while encouraging firms to reorganise work around complementarity between AI and employees rather than relying only on labour substitution.

For comparison purposes, the program could be piloted at approximately $300–500 million over three years. This is an illustrative analytical assumption rather than an existing government costing and would need to be refined using expected firm participation, grant size and administrative costs.

### Option 3: National AI Labour-Market Early Warning System
This option would establish an expanded AI labour-market monitoring system led by DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia. The system would combine occupation-level employment, vacancy, wage and training data with measures of AI exposure to identify emerging areas of labour-market pressure.

The policy would target occupations, industries and regions showing signs of AI-related adjustment, such as slower employment growth, changing vacancy patterns or rising demand for new skills. Rather than providing broad assistance to all workers or firms, the system would help government direct support toward areas where evidence of disruption is strongest.

The economic mechanism is improved information and policy targeting. AI-related labour-market effects are still uncertain and may differ substantially across occupations and regions. Better monitoring can reduce the risk of governments spending heavily on poorly targeted programs or responding too late to emerging adjustment problems.

A three-year expansion of existing DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia monitoring capability could be modelled at approximately $50–100 million. This is an illustrative analytical assumption rather than an existing government costing and would need to be refined using staffing, data infrastructure and evaluation requirements.

## 1. Problem Definition and Policy Context
Artificial intelligence is beginning to reshape the Australian labour market, but current evidence does not support the view that it is causing widespread technological unemployment. The Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) reported in July 2026 that there was no evidence of broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval in Australia. However, occupations with greater potential exposure to automation by generative AI had experienced slower employment growth than less-exposed occupations, although DEWR emphasised that these results were suggestive rather than definitive (DEWR 2026a).

This suggests that the immediate policy challenge is not mass job loss, but uneven adjustment across occupations, tasks and workers. Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) finds that generative AI is currently more likely to augment human work than replace it, with automation risk concentrated more heavily in routine tasks. The impact on employment therefore depends not only on exposure to AI, but also on the extent of adoption, how workplaces reorganise tasks, and whether workers are able to adapt through reskilling and redeployment (JSA 2025a).

This creates a case for targeted labour-market policy. If workers in highly exposed occupations face changing skill requirements, slower employment growth or reduced bargaining power, adjustment costs may be distributed unevenly. At the same time, poorly designed intervention could reduce the productivity benefits associated with AI adoption. DEWR therefore faces a policy problem of supporting worker transition while preserving incentives for firms to adopt productivity-enhancing technologies.

This proposal responds to that challenge in the context of the Australian Government’s 2026 response to the Inquiry into the Digital Transformation of Workplaces, which examined the use of AI and automated decision-making in Australian workplaces (DEWR 2026b).

## 2. Labour-Market Impacts of AI
### Employment and Tasks

AI is likely to affect Australian employment primarily by changing the composition of tasks within jobs rather than immediately eliminating entire occupations. This is consistent with the task-based framework discussed in ECON1626, where technology can substitute for some tasks while complementing workers in others. The effect on labour demand therefore depends on whether displacement effects are offset by productivity gains, new tasks and complementary forms of work.

Generative AI is especially relevant to routine and information-processing tasks, including drafting, summarising, data handling and administrative work. However, many occupations combine these activities with judgement, communication, physical interaction and accountability, meaning exposure to AI does not automatically imply job loss. The more likely near-term outcome is job redesign, with some tasks automated and others becoming more valuable.

### Wages and Productivity

AI may increase labour productivity by allowing workers to complete some tasks faster or at higher quality. If these productivity gains are shared with workers, wages and job quality may improve. However, the distribution of gains is uncertain. Workers whose skills strongly complement AI may benefit more than workers whose routine tasks are easier to automate, potentially widening wage differences across occupations and skill groups.

The impact on wages will also depend on bargaining power and labour-market adjustment. If workers can retrain and move into complementary roles, productivity gains may support higher earnings. If adjustment is slow, workers in more exposed occupations could face weaker wage growth or reduced hours even without outright unemployment.

### Industry Structure

AI adoption may also change industry structure by lowering the cost of information processing and administrative work. Larger firms may be able to adopt AI more quickly because they have more data, capital and specialist staff, while smaller firms may face higher implementation costs. This could increase productivity differences between firms and potentially strengthen the market position of early adopters.

At the same time, cheaper access to generative AI could lower barriers for some small businesses by reducing the cost of professional, analytical and administrative tasks. The structural effect is therefore ambiguous and will depend on how widely AI capabilities diffuse across firms.

### Distributional and Equity Effects

The labour-market effects of AI are unlikely to be evenly distributed. Workers in occupations with higher exposure to routine cognitive tasks may face greater adjustment pressure, while workers with stronger digital skills or complementary interpersonal and analytical capabilities may gain more from AI adoption. Younger workers may adapt more easily to changing technologies, while some older workers or workers with limited access to training may face higher transition costs.

Regional and firm-level differences also matter. Workers in areas with fewer alternative employers or training providers may find it harder to move into new roles. Without targeted support, the gains from AI could become concentrated among highly skilled workers, larger firms and metropolitan labour markets, while the costs of adjustment are borne more heavily by workers with fewer opportunities to retrain or relocate.
