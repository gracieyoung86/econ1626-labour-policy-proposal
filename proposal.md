# Preparing Australia's Workforce for AI-Driven Task Transformation

**Policy client:** Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR)

**Policy context:** Australia's labour-market response to AI-driven task transformation following the 2026 *AI and Employment in Australia* report and the Government's response to the Inquiry into the Digital Transformation of Workplaces.

## Executive Summary

AI is changing work in Australia, but current evidence does not show widespread AI-driven unemployment. The more immediate challenge is uneven adjustment as some workers face changing skill requirements and slower employment growth in more exposed occupations.

This proposal recommends combining targeted AI Skills and Transition Accounts with a National AI Labour-Market Early Warning System. Monitoring would identify occupations and regions experiencing the strongest adjustment pressure, while training support would help affected workers develop complementary skills.

This package links evidence directly to intervention, improves targeting and limits untargeted spending. A staged pilot with measurable employment and training outcomes would allow DEWR to expand or redesign the program as evidence develops.

## 1. Problem Definition and Policy Context

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the Australian labour market, but current evidence does not indicate widespread technological unemployment. DEWR reported in July 2026 that occupations with greater potential exposure to generative AI automation had experienced slower employment growth, although the evidence remains preliminary (DEWR 2026a).

The immediate policy challenge is therefore uneven adjustment rather than mass job loss. Jobs and Skills Australia finds that generative AI is currently more likely to augment work than replace it, with outcomes depending on adoption, task redesign and workers’ ability to reskill (JSA 2025).

This creates a case for targeted labour-market policy. Workers in highly exposed occupations may face changing skill requirements or weaker employment growth, while overly broad intervention could reduce the productivity benefits of AI adoption. This proposal responds to that challenge within the Australian Government’s 2026 response to the Inquiry into the Digital Transformation of Workplaces (DEWR 2026b).

## 2. Labour-Market Impacts of AI

### Employment and Tasks

AI is more likely to change tasks within jobs than immediately eliminate entire occupations. Some activities may be automated while others are complemented by AI, so employment effects depend on whether displacement is offset by productivity gains, new tasks and demand for complementary skills (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2019; JSA 2025; ILO 2025).

### Wages and Productivity

AI can raise productivity by helping workers complete tasks faster or at higher quality, but gains may be uneven. Workers whose skills complement AI may benefit, while workers concentrated in routine cognitive tasks may face weaker wage growth if adaptation is slow (Productivity Commission 2025).

### Industry Structure

AI may widen productivity differences between firms. Large firms may adopt faster because they have more capital, data and specialist staff, while smaller firms face higher implementation costs. However, generative AI may also reduce some administrative and professional costs for SMEs.

### Distributional and Equity Effects

Workers with stronger digital and complementary skills may gain more, while those with limited access to training may face greater transition costs. Regional workers may also have fewer employers or training providers. Without targeted support, AI-related gains could become concentrated among higher-skilled workers, larger firms and metropolitan labour markets (JSA 2025).

## 3. Policy Options

### Option 1: AI Skills and Transition Accounts

Provide targeted training credits to workers in occupations experiencing significant AI-related task change. Funding could cover accredited short courses or vocational training in AI literacy, digital skills and complementary human capabilities. The policy would reduce retraining costs and improve worker mobility. A three-year pilot is assumed at about $250–400 million, based on support for roughly 40,000–60,000 workers plus administration and evaluation. This is an analytical assumption, not an existing government costing.

### Option 2: Employer Co-Investment for AI Adoption and Worker Training

Provide support to firms adopting AI, conditional on employee training, consultation and redeployment assistance. The policy would mainly target SMEs facing higher adoption costs and capability constraints. It aims to encourage productivity-enhancing AI adoption while reducing underinvestment in worker skills. A three-year pilot is assumed at about $300–500 million. This is an analytical assumption rather than an existing government costing.

### Option 3: National AI Labour-Market Early Warning System

Expand DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia monitoring using employment, vacancies, wages and AI-exposure indicators. The system would identify occupations and regions showing emerging adjustment pressure, allowing support to be targeted more precisely. A three-year expansion is assumed at about $50–100 million for staffing, data infrastructure and evaluation. This is an analytical assumption rather than an existing government costing.

## 4. Policy Options Evaluation

| Criterion | Option 1: AI Skills and Transition Accounts | Option 2: Employer Co-Investment | Option 3: Early Warning System |
|---|---|---|---|
| Efficiency | High – directly improves worker skills and mobility | High – supports productivity-enhancing AI adoption and training | Moderate – improves targeting but does not directly fund adjustment |
| Equity | High – can prioritise vulnerable and lower-income workers | Moderate – benefits depend on employer participation | High – can identify disadvantaged occupations and regions |
| Feasibility | High – could use existing training and VET infrastructure | Moderate – requires grant design, monitoring and employer compliance | High – builds on existing DEWR and JSA data systems |
| Fiscal Cost | Moderate-high – approx. $250–400m over three years | Moderate-high – approx. $300–500m over three years | Low-moderate – approx. $50–100m over three years |
Option 1 performs strongly on equity because it supports workers directly, but it has a relatively high fiscal cost. Option 2 may generate strong productivity gains, but access could favour firms already capable of applying for support. Option 3 is the cheapest and easiest to implement, but it does not itself provide adjustment assistance.

The options therefore address different problems: Option 1 supports worker transition, Option 2 supports firm adoption, and Option 3 improves policy targeting. Combining Options 1 and 3 provides a tighter package by using labour-market evidence to direct training support where adjustment pressures are strongest.

## 5. Recommended Policy Package

The recommended package combines Option 1 with Option 3. The Early Warning System would identify occupations, industries and regions showing the strongest evidence of AI-related adjustment, while Skills and Transition Accounts would provide targeted retraining support.

This approach improves fiscal efficiency by linking assistance to observed labour-market pressure rather than providing broad subsidies. DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia would update priority occupations as evidence changes.

## 6. Implementation Plan

| Action | Responsible Agency | Timeline | Indicative Cost | KPI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Build AI labour-market monitoring framework and identify priority occupations | DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia | Months 1–6 | $15–25m | Priority occupation list and dashboard published |
| Launch targeted Skills and Transition Account pilot | DEWR, state training authorities and training providers | Months 6–18 | $100–150m | Enrolment and completion rates |
| Expand or redesign the program based on evidence | DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia | Years 2–3 | $150–225m | Employment transitions and movement into growing or AI-complementary roles |
| Independent evaluation | DEWR-appointed evaluator | End of Year 3 | $10–15m | Evaluation completed and recommendations implemented |

Implementation should begin with monitoring. During the first six months, DEWR and Jobs and Skills Australia would identify occupations showing the strongest adjustment pressure before launching a targeted training pilot.

Expansion should depend on pilot results. KPIs should include training completion, post-training employment, wage outcomes and regional participation. This staged approach limits fiscal risk and allows adjustment as evidence develops.

## 7. Risks and Mitigations

AI exposure does not always lead to job displacement, so eligibility should combine exposure measures with observed employment, vacancy and wage indicators. Low participation could be reduced through flexible delivery and regional support.

Regular review of eligible courses would reduce mismatch with employer demand, while a staged pilot and independent evaluation would limit fiscal risk before wider expansion.
